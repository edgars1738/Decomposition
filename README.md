# Decomposition

## This assignment performs different types of decomposition, such as STL, Seasonally Adjusted, Classical Decomposition, Additive and Multiplicative Decomposition

- Vehicle sales move in long waves rather than following a steady upward trend. They fall sharply during bad economic times, such as the early 1980s, 2008-09, and 2020, and then recover. On top of those waves is a smaller pattern that repeats every year.
- The trend shows sales drop during recessions and recovers afterward, and they have been fairly level in recent years. The seasonal part is a small pattern that repeats each year. The remainder is what is leftover, and it has large spikes around 2001, 2009, and 2020. These were unusual events, such as sales incentives and the pandemic, that the trend and seasonal parts could not explain.
- The adjusted line looks almost the same as the original because the yearly pattern is small compared with the larger swings. The big drops and jumps are still there, which shows they came from real events in the economy and not from the time of year.
- The forecast expects sales to stay close to their recent level, with the usual yearly pattern repeating. The shaded range gets wider further into the future, which shows growing uncertainty. 
- This gives almost the same picture as STL. Sales tend to be highest in late summer and December and lowest in October and November, with smaller dips in spring and early summer. The largest leftover values come from 1986, 2001, and 2020, years with unusual sales events that the trend and yearly pattern cannot explain.
- This looks almost the same as the STL version. Both show the same recessions, with the sharpest decline in 2008-09. The brief jump in August 2009 stands out due to the Cash for Clunkers program.
- The yearly pattern is small compared with the long-term ups and downs, so unusual events matter more than the time of year. The additive model fits reasonably well because the yearly pattern stays about the same size whether sales are high or low.
- The seasonal shape and the trend are nearly identical to the additive model. The yearly pattern does not grow when sales are higher, so the multiplicative model is not clearly better, and either one describes this data well. The same unusual years, especially 2009 and 2020, still show up as the largest leftovers.
